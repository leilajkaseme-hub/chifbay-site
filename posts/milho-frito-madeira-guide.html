What Is Milho Frito? Madeira's Fried Cornmeal Side Dish, Explained

9 September 20266 min readFunchal · Madeira

Milho frito is Madeira's fried cornmeal side dish — dense cubes with a crisp fried shell, cooked with garlic and collard greens, and served at almost every table that also serves espetada. Here's what it actually is, how it's made, and where to find it.

What is milho frito?

Milho frito, literally "fried corn" in Portuguese, is a Madeiran side dish of firm, chilled cornmeal cut into small cubes and deep-fried until the outside turns crisp and golden while the inside stays soft and dense. It sits somewhere between polenta and a savoury fritter, and it's one of the few dishes on the island that almost never appears alone — it's built to go alongside grilled meat.

What is milho frito made of?

The base is white cornmeal, cooked slowly with water, lard or butter, crushed garlic and shredded couve, the loose-leafed Portuguese collard green, until the mixture thickens into something close to a very firm polenta. That mixture — called milho cozido, or "cooked corn," in its unfried form — is left to cool and set for a couple of hours, then cut into cubes and fried in hot oil until each piece has a crisp shell.

Is milho frito the same as polenta?

Not quite. Both start as cooked cornmeal set firm enough to slice, but Italian polenta is usually grilled or pan-fried in slabs, while milho frito is denser, mixed with garlic and collard greens before it sets, and always cut into small cubes before deep-frying. The greens and garlic give it a savoury, slightly bitter edge that plain polenta doesn't have, and the smaller cube shape means far more crisp surface per bite.

Why is milho frito always served with espetada?

Espetada — chunks of beef seasoned with garlic and bay leaf, skewered and grilled over wood, traditionally bay-laurel — is Madeira's signature main, and milho frito is its standard side almost everywhere on the island. The pairing works because the fried cornmeal soaks up the meat's garlic-and-wine juices at the table, doing the same job bread or potatoes do elsewhere, but with a crunch that plain starch doesn't offer.

Where did milho frito come from?

Corn arrived in Madeira with Portuguese settlers in the 15th century and adapted well to the island's steep, terraced plots, quickly becoming a staple alongside potatoes and bread in rural households. Milho frito grew out of a practical habit rather than a recipe book: leftover cornmeal porridge, once it had cooled and firmed up overnight, was cut and fried rather than reheated soft, and the crisp result became a dish in its own right.

Where can you try milho frito in Madeira?

Nearly any restaurant serving espetada will bring milho frito alongside it without being asked, since the two are treated as a set rather than separate menu items. The dish is especially associated with Câmara de Lobos and Estreito de Câmara de Lobos, the fishing and wine-growing villages west of Funchal where open-air churrascarias grill espetada over wood skewers hung above the table.

Can you find it near a boat trip along the coast?

Yes — Câmara de Lobos, one of the villages most associated with milho frito and espetada, is the first stop on Chifbay's Day Trip from Funchal, so a morning by boat along the coast pairs naturally with a late lunch in the village afterwards. It's a good way to bookend a day: cliffs and coves from the water first, then the island's most traditional meal on dry land.

Espetada gets the attention. Milho frito is the reason the plate never feels finished without it.

If espetada is the dish everyone orders in Madeira, milho frito is the one nobody thinks to order separately — it just turns up, crisp-edged and unmissable, and quietly does half the work of the meal.

Frequently asked questions

What is milho frito made of?
Cornmeal cooked with water, lard or butter, garlic and collard greens until firm, then chilled, cubed and deep-fried until crisp.

Is milho frito the same as polenta?
Similar base, but denser, seasoned with garlic and greens, and always fried into small crisp cubes rather than grilled slabs.

What is milho frito usually served with?
Espetada, Madeira's skewered grilled beef — the two are treated as a single classic pairing.

Where did milho frito come from?
It developed from leftover cornmeal porridge after corn became a staple crop on Madeira's terraces in the 15th century.

Where can you try milho frito in Madeira?
Most traditional restaurants serve it with espetada, especially the churrascarias of Câmara de Lobos and Estreito de Câmara de Lobos.
