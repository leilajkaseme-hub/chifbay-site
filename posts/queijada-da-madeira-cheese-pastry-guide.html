What Is Queijada da Madeira? The Island's Goat-Cheese Pastry, Explained

13 September 20266 min readFunchal · Madeira

Next to poncha and bolo do caco, queijada da Madeira rarely gets a mention outside the island — which is a shame, because it's one of the oldest sweets still made here. Here's what it actually is, what makes it different from the Sintra version most visitors already know, and where to find one that's worth eating.

What is queijada da Madeira?

It's a small, flat baked pastry — about 7 to 8cm across and roughly a centimetre thick — made from a thin dough shell folded loosely around a filling of fresh goat's-milk curd cheese, sugar and eggs. The dough itself is simple: flour, butter, sugar and water, rolled thin so the filling stays the star. Bite into one and the texture is soft and slightly grainy rather than smooth, with a tang from the goat cheese that keeps it from tasting like a plain sugar tart. It's traditionally eaten in a couple of bites, standing at a pastelaria counter with a coffee, not plated with a fork.

What's actually in the filling?

Fresh goat's-milk curd cheese, made by curdling the milk without ageing it, mixed with sugar, eggs and a little flour to bind it before baking. That curd is the whole point — it's what separates queijada da Madeira from nearly every other Portuguese pastry called by the same name. Bakers fold the edges of the dough up and over the filling by hand rather than pressing it into a fixed mould, which is why no two queijadas look quite identical: a slightly irregular, hand-finished shape is part of what marks out a proper one from a factory-made copy.

How is it different from queijadas de Sintra or the Azores version?

They share a name and a shared root in convent baking, but the resemblance mostly stops there. Queijadas de Sintra, the version most visitors to mainland Portugal already know, use cow's-milk cheese with cinnamon in a flakier, more buttery crust. The Azorean queijada skips the pastry shell entirely, baking the custard directly in a small paper or tin case. Madeira's version sits in between — a plain dough, no cinnamon, and a distinctly tangier filling from the goat's-milk curd. A manuscript from Funchal's Convent of the Incarnation, published in the island's Historical Archive in 1937, traces the recipe back to the same monastic confectionery tradition that produced most of Portugal's classic sweets, which is how a single pastry name ended up meaning three genuinely different things across the country.

Where can you try an authentic queijada da Madeira?

Pastelarias around Funchal carry them, and you'll find stalls selling them at the Mercado dos Lavradores alongside the flower ladies and the fruit stands. But the freshest ones tend to come from smaller neighbourhood bakeries rather than the tourist-facing cafés on the main squares — worth asking at whichever village you happen to be passing through, since goat cheese is still made close to home in several of Madeira's rural parishes. They keep well for a day or two, which makes them an easy thing to pick up before heading down to the marina.

What should you drink with it?

A small glass of Madeira wine — a medium-sweet Bual works particularly well — sits nicely against the tang of the cheese, and it holds its own against poncha or a strong espresso too, which is how most locals actually eat it. We keep a similar spread of local snacks and drinks aboard on our Day Trip and Sunset Trip, so if queijada doesn't make it onto your itinerary on land, there's usually something close to it in spirit waiting on the boat.

It's not trying to impress you. It's just been made the same way for a very long time.

Queijada da Madeira won't show up on many souvenir lists, and that's part of its appeal — it's a pastry Madeirans actually eat, not one built for visitors. If you spot one in a bakery window, it's worth the detour.
