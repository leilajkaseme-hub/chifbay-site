Top 10 Souvenirs to Bring Home From Madeira

8 September 20267 min readFunchal · Madeira

Half of what's sold as a "Madeira souvenir" on Funchal's tourist strip is imported from mainland Portugal or further afield. These ten are the real thing — grown, woven, distilled or stitched on the island — ranked by how easy they are to actually bring home, and where to find the genuine version instead of the copy.

1. Madeira wine — the one bottle worth the extra suitcase weight

Madeira wine is the single souvenir most tied to the island: a fortified wine deliberately heated during ageing, a process called estufagem or the slower canteiro method, which is also why it's one of the only wines in the world that survives weeks after opening without spoiling. Buy from a producer's own lodge in Funchal's old town — Blandy's, Barbeito and Henriques & Henriques all run tastings — rather than the airport, where the range is thin and the prices aren't better.

2. Bordado Madeira — hand embroidery with proof it's genuine

Bordado Madeira, the island's traditional whitework embroidery, is stitched entirely by hand on linen, organdie or cotton, and authentic pieces carry a small lead seal from IBTAM, the official institute that certifies where and how each item was made. Tablecloths, handkerchiefs and christening gowns sold without that seal on open-air stalls are almost always machine-made imports — buy from a specialist embroidery house in central Funchal instead.

3. Vime — wickerwork woven in Camacha

Most of the wicker sold across Madeira, from baskets to full armchairs, is still woven in Camacha, a hillside village above Funchal that's built its economy around willow work since the 19th century and also builds the wooden-and-wicker sledges used on the Monte toboggan run. The village's own wicker workshop sells the freshest range and ships larger pieces if you can't fit a chair in your carry-on.

4. A poncha kit — aguardente de cana, honey and the wooden pilão

A bottle of aguardente de cana, Madeira's raw sugarcane spirit, plus honey and lemon and the wooden pilão used to muddle them, is the easiest way to recreate poncha, the island's signature drink, back home. Câmara de Lobos, where the drink is traditionally said to have originated, still sells the components together in small tasca shops near the harbour.

5. Bolo de mel — a cake that's built to survive the trip

Bolo de mel, Madeira's dense spiced cake bound with cane molasses rather than actual honey, was traditionally baked to keep for months without refrigeration, which makes it one of the few local foods that genuinely survives a long flight home. It's sold whole or in smaller wrapped portions at Mercado dos Lavradores and most Funchal bakeries.

6. Mel-de-cana — the "honey" that's actually cane syrup

Mel-de-cana is the thick, dark sugarcane syrup that gives poncha and bolo de mel their name and sweetness, despite containing no bee honey at all. It's sold bottled in most Funchal grocers and markets and keeps indefinitely, making it a lighter, cheaper alternative to a full bottle of spirit for anyone short on luggage weight.

7. Fruit liqueurs — maracujá, anona and banana

Madeira's warm south-coast terraces grow passion fruit (maracujá), custard apple (anona) and the island's own small, sweet banana, and all three turn up as liqueurs on the same shelves as the wine lodges. They're lower-proof and cheaper than a bottle of Madeira wine, which makes them an easy add-on rather than the main gift.

8. Strelitzia — the flower boxed for the flight home

Madeira calls itself the Island of Flowers, and the strelitzia, or bird of paradise, is its signature bloom — sold at flower stalls near Mercado dos Lavradores in flight-ready boxes designed to survive the journey and last for weeks once potted. It's one of the few living souvenirs airlines are used to seeing checked in.

9. Hand-painted azulejo house signs and tiles

Small hand-painted ceramic tiles, often made to order with a street name, house number or a scene of Funchal's rooftops, are sold by a handful of workshops in the old town and take a few days to fire and finish. They're heavier than embroidery or a liqueur bottle but pack flat, which makes them easier to carry than they look.

10. A wicker toboggan miniature — from Monte's most famous ride

Small wicker-and-wood models of the carros de cesto, the basket sledges that carreiros still steer down from Monte to Funchal, are made by the same Camacha workshops that build the full-size versions. They're a compact, unmistakably Madeiran way to bring home a ride most visitors only experience once.

If it isn't stitched, woven, distilled or grown on the island, it isn't really a Madeira souvenir — it's just something that happened to be for sale there.

Pack the wine and liqueurs in checked luggage, keep the embroidery and tiles in your carry-on where they won't get crushed, and leave room — most of this list is easier to find in central Funchal than at the airport on your way out.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most authentic souvenir to buy in Madeira?
A bottle of Madeira wine, since the fortified, heat-aged style is unique to the island; certified bordado Madeira embroidery is the next most distinctive.

How do you know if Madeira embroidery is genuine?
Look for the small lead IBTAM seal — pieces without it on tourist stalls are almost always machine-made imports.

Can you bring Madeira wine home in your luggage?
Yes, in checked luggage without special restriction. In hand luggage, standard international limits on liquids over 100ml still apply.

Is it better to buy souvenirs in Funchal or at the airport?
Funchal — specialist shops and wine lodges carry a far wider range than the airport's handful of duty-free counters, at similar or better prices.

What souvenir from Madeira travels best?
Bolo de mel, traditionally baked to keep for months without refrigeration, which means it survives a long flight home undamaged.
