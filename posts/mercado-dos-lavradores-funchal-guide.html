Mercado dos Lavradores

30 August 20267 min readFunchal · Madeira

Every guidebook tells you to visit Funchal's market. Fewer tell you which floor to head for first, which stalls to skip, or that the whole building is a piece of 1940s civic design worth looking at even if you buy nothing. Here's what Mercado dos Lavradores actually is, and how to see it properly.

What is the Mercado dos Lavradores, and why is it Funchal's most famous market?

It's Funchal's permanent covered market for fruit, flowers, vegetables and fish, built in 1940 to give the city's farmers and fishermen a proper trading hall instead of open-air stalls. The Art Deco building was designed by architect Edmundo Tavares, and its facade, main entrance and fish hall are lined with azulejo tile panels by João Rodrigues depicting market life and regional scenes — reason enough on its own to walk in, even if you're not buying anything. Its name translates simply as "market of the farm workers," and more than eighty years on it's still a working market, not a museum piece, a few minutes' walk from the Zona Velha old town.

What will you actually find inside?

The ground floor and central courtyard are given over to fruit, vegetables and flowers, with stalls stacked high with produce most visitors have never seen before — custard apple (anona), tamarillo, passion fruit, papaya and Madeira's own small, sweet banana alongside more familiar mangoes and avocados. Growers from across the island bring produce in daily, so the mix shifts a little with the season, but the sheer density of colour and smell is the point: this is what a subtropical Atlantic island actually grows, laid out on one floor.

Who are the flower ladies, and why do they wear regional dress?

They're florists carrying on one of the market's oldest traditions — women selling bunches of strelitzia, orchids and proteas while dressed in the "saia à Madeirense," the island's brightly coloured, embroidered traditional costume with a red-and-yellow striped skirt and pointed cap. It's one of the most photographed corners of the market, and while a few stalls now lean into that for the cameras, the flower trade itself is genuinely old — Madeira's flower export industry has centred on this building for generations.

What's downstairs in the fish market?

A different world entirely. The lower level, tiled floor to ceiling in blue-and-white azulejos, is Funchal's fish market — the place to see whole tuna and Madeira's signature deep-sea catch, the black scabbardfish (espada), hauled up from as deep as 800 metres by local boats and laid out on marble slabs each morning. It's louder, colder and less curated for tourists than upstairs, and worth the walk down even if you're not cooking dinner — it's the clearest picture on the island of what actually comes out of the Atlantic here.

When should you visit — and when should you avoid it?

Early morning on a Friday or Saturday is the sweet spot, ideally before 10am, when local growers' stalls are at their freshest and the fish hall is in full swing. The market opens Monday to Friday from around 7am to 7pm and Saturday from 7am to 2pm, closing entirely on Sundays and public holidays. By mid-morning on cruise ship days the ground floor fills fast, so an early visit — or a later afternoon one on a weekday — gets you a much quieter version of the same market.

Are the prices really higher for tourists?

At some stalls, yes. The fixed fruit displays closest to the main entrance are known for charging noticeably more than stalls further inside or on the upper gallery, which cater more to locals doing their weekly shop. Ask the price before you buy anything, and don't feel awkward walking past the first row to compare — it's standard practice, not rudeness. Many vendors also offer free tastes of exotic fruit to draw you in; they're often intensely sweet, which is exactly the point, so try a little before committing to a bag.

How does the market fit into a wider day exploring Madeira's coast?

The tuna and espada on those marble slabs are landed by small boats working the same waters a private boat trip with Chifbay covers on the way past Câmara de Lobos — the market is where that catch ends up a few hours later. Pairing a morning at the market with an afternoon on the water gives you both ends of Madeira's food chain in a single day: where the fish is sold, and where it's actually caught.

Skip it and you'll still have a good Madeira trip. Walk it properly — both floors, an early start, a few honest questions about price — and you'll understand more about how the island eats than almost anywhere else you could spend an hour in Funchal.

Frequently asked questions

What time does Mercado dos Lavradores open?
Monday to Friday roughly 7am–7pm, Saturday 7am–2pm — closed Sundays and public holidays.

Is it open on Sundays?
No, the market is closed every Sunday and on public holidays.

What's the best time to visit?
Friday or Saturday morning before 10am, ahead of the fish hall's busiest hours and the cruise-ship crowds.

Do vendors overcharge tourists?
Some fixed stalls near the entrance do — ask prices first and compare stalls further inside or upstairs.

What food can you try there?
Free tastings of exotic fruit upstairs, plus fresh tuna and espada in the tiled fish hall downstairs.
