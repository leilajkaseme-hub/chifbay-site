What Is Aguardente de Cana? Madeira's Sugarcane Rum, Explained

3 September 20266 min readFunchal · Madeira

Aguardente de cana is Madeira's oldest spirit — a clear, fiery sugarcane rum distilled on the island since the 15th century, long before poncha turned it into a cocktail. Here's what it actually is, how it differs from the rum most people know, and where you can watch it made.

What is aguardente de cana?

Aguardente de cana is a spirit distilled from fermented sugarcane juice, produced on Madeira since sugarcane was first planted on the island in the 1400s. Under its official name, "Rum da Madeira — Rum Agrícola," it holds Protected Geographical Indication status, meaning it can only be made in the Madeira archipelago, exclusively from sugarcane grown there, and only by fermenting and distilling the raw cane juice — no molasses, no shortcuts. The result is a clear, high-proof spirit with a sharp, grassy edge that's unmistakably different from the rum sold in most supermarkets.

Is aguardente de cana just rum with a different name?

Not quite. Almost all mass-market rum — Caribbean or otherwise — is made from molasses, the thick, dark byproduct left over after sugar has already been extracted and refined out of the cane. Aguardente de cana skips that step entirely: the cane is pressed and its juice fermented and distilled the same day it's cut, before the sugars have a chance to degrade. This "agricultural rum" method — the same family as French rhum agricole from Martinique — produces a spirit that tastes more like the plant itself: vegetal, slightly peppery, and less sweet than a molasses rum of the same age.

Is aguardente de cana the same thing as poncha?

No — poncha is what you get after aguardente de cana has been mixed with something else. Poncha beats the raw spirit with honey and citrus (traditionally lemon) until it froths, softening the alcohol into Madeira's best-known drink. Drunk on its own, without the honey and citrus, aguardente de cana is a different experience entirely — closer to a strong, clear agricultural rum, traditionally taken as a short, bracing shot rather than sipped.

Where is Madeira's rum actually made?

Only a handful of working sugar mills remain on the island, and the most visitor-friendly is Engenhos do Norte in Porto da Cruz, on the north coast. Built in 1927 and still running on its original 19th-century machinery, it's one of the last places in Europe where sugarcane is still processed the traditional way, cane cart to still. Entry is free, and a self-guided route walks you past the crushing rollers, the fermentation tanks and the copper stills, ending at the Casa do Rum next door for a tasting. The mill only actually presses cane during the harvest, roughly February to May — outside that window you'll see the machinery standing still, but the building and the history are there year-round.

Who is William Hinton, and why is the name on so many bottles?

William Hinton was a British-Madeiran distiller who founded his rum house on the island in 1845 and, by the 1880s, had built one of the region's dominant sugarcane and rum operations. The original mill closed in 1986, but two decades later a descendant, Jimmy Welsh, revived the family's know-how at the Engenho Novo in Calheta on the south coast, ageing rum in French oak casks rather than selling it young and clear. What began with 90 barrels in 2009 has grown into a cellar of well over a thousand, and the William Hinton name — both the clear, young "Rum Agrícola" and the darker aged versions — is now one of the easiest to find in shops across Funchal.

Can you visit a working sugar mill in Madeira?

Yes, and it's one of the more overlooked things to do on the island — most visitors head straight for the poncha bars in Câmara de Lobos without ever seeing where the spirit inside the glass actually comes from. Beyond Engenhos do Norte, the Engenho Novo in Calheta runs its own tours and tastings focused on the aged rums, giving a very different picture of the same raw spirit. Seeing both — the working 1927 mill on the north coast and the barrel cellar on the south coast — makes the drink in your poncha glass make a lot more sense.

How should you drink it?

Neat and cold is the traditional way locals take the clear, unaged version — a short, sharp shot rather than something to linger over. The aged versions, spent a few years in oak, are closer to a good dark rum and are usually taken slowly, the way you'd drink a whisky. Either way, it's rarely drunk completely alone in company: a bottle tends to appear alongside poncha, cheese and conversation, which is exactly how we serve it aboard — a splash of aguardente de cana in freshly made poncha, glass in hand, on the way out to Cabo Girão on a Chifbay Day Trip.

Poncha gets all the attention. The spirit that makes it — and Madeira's oldest export before wine ever was — is worth knowing on its own terms.

Whether you taste it at a working mill, an aged-rum cellar, or from a chilled bottle on board, aguardente de cana is one of the few things left on the island made almost exactly the way it was 500 years ago.
