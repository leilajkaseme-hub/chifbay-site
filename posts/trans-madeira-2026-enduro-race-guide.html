Trans Madeira 2026: The Island's 6-Day Enduro Race

28 September 20266 min readFunchal · Madeira

While most visitors this week are booking levada walks and sunset cruises, a few hundred riders are pinning numbers to their handlebars for one of the toughest weeks on Madeira's sporting calendar. Here's what Trans Madeira actually is, when it runs, and whether it changes anything about your own trip.

What is Trans Madeira?

Trans Madeira is a six-day, timed enduro mountain bike stage race across Madeira's mountain and coastal trails, run under the name Trans Madeira powered by Scott. It links more than 30 special stages over roughly 250km of trail and liaison riding, mixing sponsored enduro professionals with strong amateur entries from across Europe's mountain biking scene. Unlike a pure downhill event, riders also pedal significant stretches between the timed sections, so six days of endurance matters almost as much as raw technical skill.

When does the 2026 edition run?

The Autumn 2026 edition runs from 28 September to 3 October 2026, the second of two editions held on the island that year after a Summer edition in May. The later date isn't an accident — by the end of September the sharpest summer heat has eased on the exposed upper trails, while the ground is normally still dry enough to hold grip before the wetter winter months set in.

What's the route actually like?

The six days move across the island's north, south, east and west, stringing together more than 30 special stages over the week rather than repeating one forest loop. Exact course details for each stage are typically kept close to the organizers' chest until race week itself, but past editions have included alpine sections above Funchal and Câmara de Lobos on the south coast alongside remoter trail further north and west — a route built to show off how much the terrain changes across a fairly small island, from dry volcanic ridgeline to damp laurel forest.

Who actually races it?

Trans Madeira sits at the tougher end of enduro stage racing. Daily distances run 35 to 60km including liaisons, and the trails range from flowing forest singletrack to loose, exposed ridgelines where a mistake has real consequences. It draws sponsored enduro professionals using it as a season highlight alongside strong amateurs who've trained specifically for six consecutive days of racing — it isn't a casual weekend event, and isn't really built for a first-time enduro rider.

Is it the same as Rali da Madeira?

No, and the two get confused mostly because they share the island and the word "rally" or "race." Rali da Madeira is a closed-road car rally that shuts sections of the public road network for its timed stages, usually tied to the autumn grape harvest. Trans Madeira is a mountain bike race that runs almost entirely on off-road hiking and biking trails and rarely touches a driveable road at all. Both are competitive stage-race formats on the same island, but they create very different disruption for anyone just visiting.

Can you watch it as a visitor?

Not easily, and that's part of its character. Most special stages run on narrow mountain trail far from parking or a public viewpoint, so it isn't set up as a roadside spectator sport the way a closed-road rally is. The realistic way to catch any of it is near a stage start or finish area, or through the organizers' own daily coverage, rather than planning to line a trail with a coffee.

Does it change anything about a normal week in Madeira?

Barely. Because the race stays on hiking and biking trails rather than the public road network, it has little effect on driving times, ferry schedules or coastal activity during race week — a real contrast with the road closures a car rally brings. If you're on the island this week for anything else, from a levada walk to time on the water, race week shouldn't reshape your plans.

What's the easier way to see the island this same week?

Trade the mountain for the coast. If six days of alpine enduro racing isn't your idea of a Madeira trip, the same week works well for the opposite pace — a private boat trip with Chifbay along the south coast covers Cabo Girão, Fajã dos Padres and the calm water below the very cliffs the racers are riding above, with no bike fitness, technical trail skill or number pinned to your back required.

One island, two very different ways to spend six days on it.

Trans Madeira is a genuinely serious event for the riders taking part in it, but for everyone else on the island that week it's mostly background noise — a reminder of how much terrain Madeira packs into one small volcanic outline, whether you're crossing it on two wheels or watching the same cliffs go by from the deck of a boat.
